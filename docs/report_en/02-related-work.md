## 2. Related Work

This section explains which existing results this method builds on, and the division of labour with them. **The citations below are positional pointers by author and core idea, meant for lookup by name — they are not a formally vetted reference list**; this codebase has no citation manager, so exact versions and page numbers should be checked against the originals.

### 2.1 Training dynamics carry sample-level information

The idea that "how a model treats a sample during training" is itself a signal has a fairly solid foundation. Zhang et al. (2017) showed deep networks can fully memorize random labels, meaning the loss curve already carries information about "how hard or how anomalous this sample is"; Arpit et al. (2017) showed networks tend to learn simple patterns first and memorize noise later, i.e. noise and clean samples are **separable along the time dimension**. Toneva et al. (2019) characterize sample difficulty with "forgetting events" (how many times a sample is learned and then forgotten during training); Swayamdipta et al. (2020)'s Dataset Cartography uses confidence mean and variance to map a dataset into easy / ambiguous / hard-to-learn regions.

The loss mean/last/slope/curvature, `converge_epoch`, gradient norm, and cosine similarity to a reference gradient used in this report belong to the same family of signal, differing mainly in **collection granularity**: the works above mostly use confidence or correctness statistics under standard mini-batch training, whereas here `micro_batch=1` isolates each sample's gradient individually (Section 4.3), making it possible to obtain a per-sample gradient norm and direction, not just a per-sample loss.

### 2.2 Tracing a single sample's influence via gradients

A second line of work directly quantifies "how much a given training sample influenced the model." Koh and Liang (2017) introduced influence functions to deep learning; Pruthi et al. (2020)'s TracIn approximates the same quantity using the accumulated inner product of gradients during training. This report's `cos_sim_ref` (a sample's gradient cosine similarity to the average gradient direction over 200 held-out samples) and `update_contrib` are lightweight versions of this idea: instead of an exact influence value, they only need a proxy quantity good enough for ranking, cheap enough to compute for every training sample every epoch.

### 2.3 The "small-loss" criterion in noisy-label learning

In the noisy-label literature, "samples with small loss are more trustworthy" is the mainstream criterion; Han et al. (2018)'s Co-teaching and a large body of follow-up work rest on it. This criterion matters a great deal to this report, but **the direction is reversed**: it assumes noise = hard to learn = high loss. Section 5.2 and [06b](06b-feature-signatures.md) Section 6b.4 show that for noise like templating and exact duplication, the noise is precisely the batch with the *lowest* loss and the *fastest* convergence — the small-loss criterion will systematically protect it. Feldman and Zhang (2020)'s analysis of memorization and long-tail samples, together with Carlini et al.'s work quantifying verbatim memorization in language models, supply the other half of the explanation for this reversal: highly repetitive or highly templated content gets memorized preferentially, and so shows up in training dynamics as "abnormally typical" rather than "abnormally anomalous."

### 2.4 Filtering and deduplicating instruction-tuning data

On the LLM side, "data quality matters more than quantity" already has several empirical demonstrations: Zhou et al. (2023)'s LIMA reaches competitive performance with roughly a thousand curated samples; Chen et al. (2023)'s AlpaGasus filters low-quality instruction data with an LLM-based scorer and improves performance by doing so; Li et al. (2024) filter by "instruction-following difficulty" (IFD). On deduplication, Lee et al. (2022) show that deduplicating training data materially improves language models; Abbas et al. (2023)'s SemDeDup and Sorscher et al. (2022)'s prototype-based pruning extend this idea to the semantic level.

The division of labour with this report is clear: those works mostly rely on an **external scorer** (another LLM, embedding similarity, or a heuristic rule) to judge quality, whereas this report relies only on **the traces the model itself leaves during training**, with no external judge introduced. The two are not in conflict — [06b](06b-feature-signatures.md) Sections 6b.6 and 6b.8 show that the static text-similarity feature `text_nn_sim` (belonging to the external-signal side) is stronger than the entire training-dynamics feature set for exact duplication and topic mismatch, and completely useless for garbled text.

### 2.5 Where this report sits

Taken together, this report does not claim "training dynamics is a new signal." It answers a more specific question that the literature above has not systematically addressed: **with no noise labels at all, and with no external judge, how well can per-sample training dynamics from LoRA fine-tuning separate seven mechanistically different noise types, and does cleaning based on that separation actually improve downstream performance?** Four specific choices, each mapped to one of the three research threads (see the [06-results.md](06-results.md) introduction):

- **[Thread 3]** **Label-freedom is a hard constraint**, not an option. Labels appear only on the evaluation side; no scorer ever sees them (Section 4.4). This rules out the large body of noisy-label practice that calibrates direction against a handful of known-clean samples.
- **[Thread 2]** **Report per noise mechanism**, rather than a single aggregate number. [06b](06b-feature-signatures.md) Section 6b.4 explains why pooling the report would hide phenomena as important as direction reversal.
- **[Thread 1]** **Harm ranking does not depend on detection accuracy — it goes straight to downstream evaluation.** Most of the literature above stops at "ranking quality" (detection-side metrics like AUC / P@10%). This report additionally runs 7 downstream benchmarks separately for each noise type ([06a](06a-harm-ranking.md) Section 6a.1), splitting "is this noise type easy to detect" and "does this noise type actually cause harm" into two independent questions.
- **[Thread 3]** **It has to go all the way to downstream evaluation.** Ranking quality (AUC / P@10%) does not equal cleaning benefit; [06c](06c-detectability.md) Section 6c.5 uses real retraining and evaluation to show the two can decouple, or even go in opposite directions.
